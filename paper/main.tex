\begin{abstract}
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA typically allocate adaptation capacity uniformly, or adapt it during training from gradient- or weight-derived signals. We ask a narrower question: can \emph{ante-hoc} representation drift---measured once, before any target-domain training, from a frozen source model's own representations---serve as a trustworthy prior for layer-wise rank allocation? Using RoBERTa-base trained on MNLI and evaluated under distribution shift on WANLI and ANLI (rounds 1--3), we measure layer-wise drift with two established metrics, Maximum Mean Discrepancy and Sinkhorn distance, and find three things. First, each metric is independently stable across sample sizes, but the two metrics disagree sharply with each other---significantly anti-correlated on ANLI R1/R2 (Spearman $\rho\approx-0.6$, $p<0.05$)---and this disagreement pattern is itself sensitive to a low-level pooling choice. Second, drift magnitude dissociates from zero-shot adaptation difficulty: ANLI R3 is the hardest target by accuracy yet shows the \emph{least} measured drift of all four domains. Third, under a programmatically verified, budget-matched comparison, drift-guided rank allocation shows no measurable advantage over uniform or even inverted-drift allocation. We argue these are not a failed method but a diagnostic result: ante-hoc geometric drift metrics should not be trusted as PEFT-planning signals without independent validation against ground-truth adaptation difficulty, which we outline a protocol for.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) \cite{hu2021lora} has become a standard parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) technique: pretrained weights are frozen, and a low-rank update is injected into selected weight matrices, typically at a single, fixed rank applied uniformly across every targeted layer. This uniformity is a simplifying assumption, not a principled one---different transformer layers plausibly require different amounts of adaptation capacity when a model is moved from its source domain to a new target domain, and a growing body of work on \emph{adaptive-rank} LoRA variants addresses exactly this (Sec.~\ref{sec:related}). Every such method we are aware of, however, determines its per-layer rank from a signal computed \emph{during} fine-tuning---gradient-based importance, Fisher information, or singular-value pruning---which requires running (at least partial) training before an allocation decision can be made.

This raises a narrower, previously underexplored question: can a signal computed \emph{before} any target-domain training begins---purely from how a target domain's unlabeled data looks to the frozen source model's existing representations---usefully inform where to allocate LoRA rank? We refer to such a signal as \emph{ante-hoc}, since it requires no gradient computation, no labels, and no training on the target domain at all.

We operationalize this question using two established measures of distributional difference, Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) and Sinkhorn (entropic-regularized Wasserstein) distance, computed layer-by-layer between a frozen RoBERTa-base model's representations of its source domain (MNLI \cite{williams2018mnli}) and three target domains that are known to cause substantial zero-shot degradation: WANLI \cite{liu2022wanli} and ANLI rounds 1--3 \cite{nie2020anli}. We use the resulting per-layer drift profile to allocate a fixed LoRA rank budget across layers, and compare this to uniform allocation and to randomized/inverted-drift control allocations under an identical, programmatically-verified total trainable-parameter budget.

We present this work as a diagnostic, negative-result study, not a new state-of-the-art method: our central contribution is a methodological characterization of ante-hoc drift measurement, not a claim that drift-guided allocation improves PEFT. We show that MMD and Sinkhorn disagree with each other in ways that are domain-dependent, and that drift magnitude dissociates from actual adaptation difficulty on at least one benchmark; a first application of our verified allocation pipeline to real LoRA training, reported here as a diagnostic sanity check rather than a headline result, shows no detectable advantage for drift-guided allocation over uniform or even inverted-drift allocation, a finding consistent with and reinforcing our primary methodological result that the underlying drift signal is not yet trustworthy. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A reproducible, sample-size-stable methodology for measuring layer-wise representation drift between a source and target domain, using a frozen, task-fine-tuned transformer's own representations.
\item An empirical finding that two established distributional-distance metrics (MMD, Sinkhorn) disagree with each other in a way that is not uniform across target domains, and that this disagreement pattern is sensitive to representation-pooling methodology.
\item An empirical finding that drift magnitude does not monotonically track zero-shot adaptation difficulty, with an explanation grounded in how the benchmarks were constructed.
\item A verified, budget-matched layer-wise rank-allocation algorithm and training pipeline, including a programmatic confirmation that PEFT's automatic classifier-head handling must be accounted for to make parameter-budget-matched comparisons valid.
\item A diagnostic sanity-check application of this pipeline to real LoRA training, consistent with our central finding that ante-hoc drift metrics are not yet reliable enough to trust as an adaptation-planning signal.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Low-rank and adaptive-rank PEFT}
LoRA \cite{hu2021lora} freezes pretrained weights and learns a low-rank additive update at a single rank applied uniformly across targeted layers. AdaLoRA \cite{zhang2023adalora} was, to our knowledge, the first method to allocate rank non-uniformly, parameterizing updates via a singular-value decomposition and pruning singular values by an importance score computed during training. A number of subsequent methods extend this idea with different in-training signals: La-LoRA \cite{gu2025lalora} uses a dynamic, contribution-driven parameter budget; LAARA \cite{tripathi2026laara} uses lightweight diagonal Fisher-information estimates; ElaLoRA \cite{chang2025elalora} elastically prunes and expands rank during training; BaRA \cite{duan2026bara} performs Bayesian inference over per-layer importance; and SR-LoRA \cite{zhang2025srlora} uses the stable rank of the frozen pretrained weight matrices themselves as a static prior. In every case, the allocation signal is either computed during fine-tuning or derived from the model's weights alone, independent of the target domain's data distribution. None of these methods allocate rank from a pre-computed measurement of how the target domain's own data distribution differs from the source domain's---which is the signal this work investigates.

\subsection{Distributional distance metrics for domain shift}
MMD and the Wasserstein/optimal-transport distance are both established tools for quantifying and minimizing domain divergence \cite{shen2017wasserstein,su2019wasserstein}, typically as an adversarial training objective to be minimized during representation learning, rather than as a diagnostic measurement taken before training. Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2020rethinkmmd} specifically caution that standard MMD-based domain adaptation can be insensitive to certain real distributional shifts, a concern our own finding of MMD/Sinkhorn disagreement is consistent with. Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) \cite{kornblith2019cka} is a further candidate similarity measure, but was designed to compare representation \emph{spaces} (e.g., two layers, possibly from different networks) on the \emph{same} input data; using it to compare the same layer's representations across \emph{different} data, as a domain-shift measure, repurposes a tool whose explicit invariances (to rotation and isotropic scaling) may under-report exactly the kind of shift we wish to detect. For this reason we do not use CKA as a primary signal in this work, though we discuss it further as a candidate for controlled validation in Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}. A complementary, more directly adaptation-theoretic alternative is the classifier-induced $\mathcal{H}$-divergence of Ben-David et al.\ \cite{bendavid2010theory}, estimable cheaply via a domain classifier trained on frozen representations; we have not yet implemented this and note it as future work.

\subsection{Transferability estimation}
A related but distinct line of work estimates how well fine-tuning will transfer to a target task or domain (e.g., LogME, H-score, and related proxy measures), typically to decide whether or which pretrained checkpoint to fine-tune. These methods generally require target labels to compute and produce a single global score per task, rather than a per-layer, label-free decomposition usable to guide where PEFT capacity should be spent. Our approach is deliberately label-free and layer-wise, positioning it as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, transferability estimation.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Source model and datasets}
We use RoBERTa-base fine-tuned on MNLI \cite{williams2018mnli} (3 epochs, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$, batch size 16, AdamW, weight decay 0.01, warmup ratio 0.10) as the frozen source model. We evaluate distribution shift against three target domains: WANLI \cite{liu2022wanli}, constructed via dataset-cartography-guided GPT-3 generation and human filtering over MultiNLI, and ANLI rounds 1--3 \cite{nie2020anli}, constructed via an iterative human-and-model-in-the-loop adversarial procedure in which annotators were shown a live target model and tasked with writing examples that fooled it, with the target model escalating in strength and context diversity across rounds.

\subsection{Representation extraction}
\label{sec:methodology-reps}
For a source sample $X$ (drawn from MNLI) and a target sample $Y$, we extract hidden states $h_\ell(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$ at every layer $\ell \in \{0,\ldots,12\}$ (embedding layer plus 12 encoder layers) of the frozen source model, using attention-mask-weighted mean pooling:
\begin{equation}
\bar h_\ell(x) = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T} m_t \, h_{\ell,t}(x)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T} m_t},
\end{equation}
where $m_t \in \{0,1\}$ is the attention mask at token position $t$ and $T$ is the (dynamically, per-batch) padded sequence length. We verified this matters: naive unweighted pooling over the full padded sequence ($\frac{1}{T}\sum_t h_{\ell,t}$) includes padding positions in the mean and is non-deterministic across batch composition, since $T$ varies with the other examples in a batch.

\subsection{Drift metrics}
\label{sec:drift-metrics}
For each layer $\ell$, let $X_\ell = \{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ and $Y_\ell = \{y_1,\ldots,y_m\}$ denote the pooled source and target representations at that layer.

\textit{1) Maximum Mean Discrepancy:} We use the biased empirical estimator of squared MMD under a Gaussian RBF kernel $k(a,b) = \exp(-\|a-b\|_2^2 / 2\sigma^2)$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^2(X_\ell,Y_\ell) = {} & \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n} k(x_i,x_j) + \frac{1}{m^2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m} k(y_i,y_j) \\
& {}- \frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m} k(x_i,y_j).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The kernel bandwidth $\sigma^2$ is set per-layer by the median heuristic over the pooled set $Z_\ell = X_\ell \cup Y_\ell$, excluding zero (self-)distances:
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2 = \mathrm{median}\left(\left\{\|z_i - z_j\|_2^2 : z_i, z_j \in Z_\ell,\ i \neq j\right\}\right).
\end{equation}

\textit{2) Sinkhorn Distance:} We L2-normalize each pooled vector, $\tilde x_i = x_i / \|x_i\|_2$, and define the cost matrix $C \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ by $C_{ij} = \|\tilde x_i - \tilde y_j\|_2$. With uniform marginals $a = \tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_n$, $b = \tfrac{1}{m}\mathbf{1}_m$, the entropic-regularized optimal transport (Sinkhorn) distance is
\begin{equation}
W_\lambda(X_\ell,Y_\ell) = \min_{P \in U(a,b)} \ \langle P, C \rangle - \lambda\, H(P),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
U(a,b) = \{P \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n \times m} : P\mathbf{1}_m = a,\ P^\top \mathbf{1}_n = b\},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
H(P) = -\sum_{i,j} P_{ij}\log P_{ij},
\end{equation}
with regularization strength $\lambda = 1.0$, solved via the Sinkhorn--Knopp matrix-scaling iteration $P^{(k)} = \mathrm{diag}(u^{(k)})\, K \,\mathrm{diag}(v^{(k)})$, $K_{ij} = \exp(-C_{ij}/\lambda)$, with $u^{(k)}, v^{(k)}$ updated by alternating projection onto the marginal constraints until convergence.

Both metrics are computed and reported entirely separately for every layer, target, and sample size; \emph{we never average or otherwise combine them into a single score}, for reasons that Sec.~\ref{sec:results} shows are empirically justified: the two metrics are sensitive to different aspects of a distributional difference (MMD primarily to mean discrepancy in a nonlinear kernel-induced feature space; Sinkhorn to the full transport cost, including higher-order shape differences), and combining them would obscure a disagreement we show is itself informative.

We note a deliberate asymmetry: Sinkhorn is computed on L2-normalized representations while MMD is computed on raw representations, with its kernel bandwidth instead set by the median heuristic. This is a standard convention for each metric individually (normalization keeps the Euclidean transport cost well-scaled for Sinkhorn; the median heuristic already adapts MMD's kernel to the data's own scale, making normalization redundant for it), but it raises a fair question: is the disagreement we report in Sec.~\ref{sec:results-c} partly a normalization artifact rather than a genuine MMD-vs-Sinkhorn disagreement? We test this directly in Sec.~\ref{sec:results-c} by recomputing MMD on the same L2-normalized representations Sinkhorn uses.

\subsection{Rank allocation algorithm}
\label{sec:allocation}
Given a drift profile $D_1,\ldots,D_{12}$ over the 12 encoder layers (the embedding layer is excluded, since no LoRA module exists there), we compute normalized weights $w_\ell = D_\ell / \sum_j D_j$ and raw ranks $\mathrm{raw}_\ell = R_{\mathrm{total}} \cdot w_\ell$, where $R_{\mathrm{total}}=96$ (matching a uniform baseline of rank 8 across 12 layers). Raw ranks are converted to integers via largest-remainder (Hamilton) apportionment, Algorithm~\ref{alg:hamilton}, then clamped to $[r_{\min}, r_{\max}] = [1, 16]$; any shortfall or surplus introduced by clamping is redistributed one unit at a time to the layer(s) furthest from their clamp bound, until $\sum_\ell r_\ell = R_{\mathrm{total}}$ exactly. LoRA $\alpha=16$ is held constant across every layer and every method (i.e., not scaled with rank), so that rank is the only variable manipulated between methods.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Hamilton (largest-remainder) rank apportionment}
\label{alg:hamilton}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE drift profile $D_1,\ldots,D_{12}$, budget $R_{\mathrm{total}}$
\STATE $w_\ell \gets D_\ell / \sum_j D_j$ for each $\ell$
\STATE $\mathrm{raw}_\ell \gets R_{\mathrm{total}} \cdot w_\ell$
\STATE $r_\ell \gets \lfloor \mathrm{raw}_\ell \rfloor$ for each $\ell$
\STATE $\mathrm{rem}_\ell \gets \mathrm{raw}_\ell - r_\ell$ \COMMENT{fractional remainder}
\STATE $k \gets R_{\mathrm{total}} - \sum_\ell r_\ell$
\STATE Sort layers by $\mathrm{rem}_\ell$ descending, ties broken by layer index
\FOR{each of the top $k$ layers in sorted order}
  \STATE $r_\ell \gets r_\ell + 1$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $r_1,\ldots,r_{12}$ \COMMENT{$\sum_\ell r_\ell = R_{\mathrm{total}}$ exactly}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This procedure is applied identically, with only $D_\ell$ differing, for: \textbf{Drift-MMD} and \textbf{Drift-Sinkhorn} (the two drift profiles), \textbf{Random} (a uniform-random partition of $R_{\mathrm{total}}$ with a fixed, logged allocation seed, independent of the training seed), and \textbf{Inverse-drift} ($D'_\ell = 1/(D_\ell+\epsilon)$, $\epsilon=10^{-8}$), so that any difference in downstream results is attributable to the allocation signal, not to the rounding/apportionment logic.

\subsection{Parameter budget verification}
\label{sec:budget-verification}
We verify, rather than assume, that every method trains the same total number of parameters. We discovered that the PEFT library's \texttt{get\_peft\_model} automatically wraps the classifier head in a trainable \texttt{modules\_to\_save} entry when \texttt{task\_type=SEQ\_CLS}, contributing 592{,}899 trainable parameters independent of the LoRA rank pattern. We confirmed this constant holds identically for both a uniform ($r{=}8$) configuration and a deliberately non-uniform allocation, and re-confirmed it on two independent PEFT library versions (0.17.0 and 0.20.0). Combined with the LoRA-only budget of $\sum_\ell 2 \times r_\ell \times 1536 = 294{,}912$ parameters (query and value projections, hidden size 768), every non-zero-shot method in this study trains exactly 887{,}811 parameters in total, verified programmatically and logged per run.

This exact, bit-for-bit equality is not achievable for our AdaLoRA baseline, and we verified this directly rather than assume it. AdaLoRA \cite{zhang2023adalora} parameterizes each adapted matrix as $P E Q$ with a learned per-rank-unit singular value $E$, and its budget mechanism (peft's \texttt{RankAllocator}) \emph{masks} values to zero as it prunes from an initial rank toward a target rank, rather than shrinking the backing tensors---confirmed directly in \texttt{peft}'s implementation, where resizing tensors to the pruned rank is explicitly disabled (``this freezes the trainable parameters and nothing gets updates''). Consequently, the raw trainable-parameter count (this paper's counting convention everywhere else) stays fixed at its initial-rank size for the entire run and never reaches a lower-rank target by shrinking. With $\mathrm{init}_r=16$ (this project's own $r_{\max}$) and $\mathrm{target}_r=8$ (matching the uniform baseline), the raw count is 1{,}183{,}107 throughout training---not comparable to 887{,}811 on this project's own terms. The closest available match is the \emph{effective} budget AdaLoRA's importance-based pruning converges to by the end of training, computed from its final rank pattern rather than counted directly: $24 \times 8 \times (2\times768+1) + 592{,}899 = 888{,}003$, 192 parameters (0.02\%) over 887{,}811, an unavoidable structural overhead from the $E$ vectors that plain LoRA does not have. We report both numbers for every AdaLoRA run rather than silently using whichever is more favorable.

\section{Experimental Setup}

All training uses the identical configuration as the uniform-LoRA baseline: learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, 3 epochs, batch size 16, target modules \{query, value\}, LoRA dropout 0.1, bias disabled, seed 42 for the results reported here. Drift profiles were computed at four sample sizes ($N \in \{250, 500, 750, 950\}$) to assess stability. The PEFT rank-allocation mechanism (Sec.~\ref{sec:allocation}) was verified against the real trained checkpoint before any training run, confirming per-layer ranks are applied exactly as specified via PEFT's \texttt{rank\_pattern} argument, that only the intended 24 modules (12 layers $\times$ \{query, value\}) receive LoRA parameters, and the parameter-budget accounting above.

\subsection{Drift profile stability}
\label{sec:results-b}
Across $N=250$ to $N=950$, the layer-wise MMD profile varies by at most 13\% (relative) and Sinkhorn by at most 1.1\%, for the same target domain, with the argmax and argmin layers unchanged across sample sizes (Fig.~\ref{fig:sinkhorn-layers} shows the Sinkhorn profile at $N{=}950$). We treat this as evidence that both metrics' \emph{relative, layer-wise ordering} is a stable, repeatable measurement at this sample-size range, while noting that stability of ranking does not by itself establish that either estimator's \emph{absolute magnitude} has converged---both are finite-sample estimators of distributional distances in a 768-dimensional space, for which convergence guarantees are stronger for MMD's U-statistic estimator (dimension-independent $O(1/\sqrt{n})$) than for the entropic-regularized Sinkhorn estimate, whose bias additionally depends on the regularization strength chosen.

Only ANLI R1's significant anti-correlation survives the pooling correction unchanged; ANLI R2 loses significance, and ANLI R3 flips sign entirely, moving from a non-significant negative correlation to a borderline-significant positive one. We interpret this as evidence that whichever drift metric is chosen is not an interchangeable implementation detail: the two metrics can disagree sharply, the disagreement is not uniform across domains, and even the \emph{direction} of disagreement can be sensitive to a representation-extraction detail (pooling) that might otherwise be treated as a minor implementation choice.

\textit{Normalization ablation.} MMD and Sinkhorn are, by convention, computed under different normalization (Sec.~\ref{sec:drift-metrics}): does the disagreement above survive when this asymmetry is removed? Table~\ref{tab:normablation} recomputes MMD on the same L2-normalized representations Sinkhorn uses and reports the resulting Spearman correlation against Sinkhorn, alongside the original (raw-MMD) correlation already shown in Table~\ref{tab:correlation}. The result is mixed, and we report it exactly as found rather than as a clean confirmation: ANLI R1's correlation, the single significant finding that survived the pooling-bug fix, does \emph{not} survive normalization-matching---it weakens from $\rho=-0.63$ ($p<0.05$) to $\rho=-0.34$ ($p=0.26$), losing significance. ANLI R2 weakens similarly ($-0.40 \to -0.18$, already non-significant either way). ANLI R3's positive correlation, by contrast, is essentially unchanged ($+0.54 \to +0.55$), and WANLI's remains non-significant throughout. We therefore revise our own claim: under matched normalization, \emph{no} target domain shows a significant MMD-Sinkhorn disagreement at the conventional $p<0.05$ threshold---the one significant result in this paper was itself sensitive to a second, independent methodological choice beyond pooling. We read this as reinforcing, not undermining, our central argument: a result this sensitive to incidental preprocessing choices is exactly the kind of signal that should not be trusted as an adaptation-planning prior without the controlled validation we propose in Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Drift magnitude dissociates from adaptation difficulty}
\label{sec:results-d}
Table~\ref{tab:driftvsdiff} reports mean drift (layers 1--12) for each target alongside its zero-shot accuracy. We attribute this dissociation to how the benchmarks were constructed rather than to a failure of either metric. ANLI's difficulty is adversarially engineered: annotators were shown a live model and explicitly tasked with finding inputs that fooled it \cite{nie2020anli}, which can produce examples that are representationally unremarkable to a frozen model while still defeating its reasoning. WANLI, by contrast, is constructed via dataset-cartography-guided generation of examples matching challenging \emph{patterns} \cite{liu2022wanli}, which plausibly produces a larger surface-level shift without a correspondingly large increase in reasoning difficulty. This suggests representational drift and adaptation difficulty are related but distinct constructs, and that benchmarks whose difficulty is adversarially constructed are a meaningful stress test for any drift-based adaptation method, rather than a case to be excluded.

\subsection{Parameter budget verification}
Verification against the trained checkpoint confirmed that per-layer ranks are applied exactly as specified by the allocator (24 of 24 modules matched their assigned rank exactly), that only the intended query/value modules in the 12 encoder layers receive LoRA parameters (24 modules found, as expected), and that the LoRA-only parameter count for a representative non-uniform allocation matched the allocator's prediction of 294{,}912 exactly once the classifier head's 592{,}899 parameters are accounted for separately (Sec.~\ref{sec:allocation}). This was independently reproduced on two PEFT library versions (0.17.0 and 0.20.0), giving us confidence the 887{,}811-parameter budget-matching claim is not an artifact of one specific library version.

\subsection{Diagnostic sanity check: application to real LoRA training}
Table~\ref{tab:peft} reports a diagnostic sanity check: a first application of each allocation method to real LoRA training on ANLI R1 and R2, at a single seed. We report this as a sanity check on our primary finding, not as an independently powered PEFT benchmark: with one seed, the exact same configuration (Drift-MMD, ANLI R1) re-run on different hardware produced a 0.5-point accuracy swing (44.3\% vs.\ 43.8\%) from re-running the identical setup alone, which is as large as or larger than every method-to-method difference in the table. No method shows a consistent advantage over uniform allocation at this sample size, and the inverse-drift control performs comparably to the drift-guided methods, which is not the pattern that would support the hypothesis that drift-guided allocation helps. This is consistent with, rather than independent of, our central diagnostic finding: if the underlying drift signal were a reliable adaptation-difficulty prior (Secs.~\ref{sec:results-c} and~\ref{sec:results-d} show it is not yet established to be), we would expect drift-guided allocation to separate from its own inverse; that it does not is exactly what the measurement-level findings predict. We do not interpret this as a definitive negative result either---one seed provides essentially no statistical power to distinguish a true null from a small real effect---and report it only to be transparent about the current state of evidence.

\section{Discussion}

The two findings in Secs.~\ref{sec:results-c} and~\ref{sec:results-d} together argue for treating ``measuring domain shift'' as a nontrivial methodological question rather than a solved preliminary step. If a researcher had chosen only MMD, or only Sinkhorn, as the ``drift'' signal for ANLI R2 before and after our pooling fix, they would have reported a significant finding that later disappeared---not because the underlying phenomenon changed, but because a representation-extraction detail changed. Similarly, a researcher assuming drift magnitude tracks adaptation difficulty would be misled specifically on ANLI R3, in a way explainable by the benchmark's adversarial construction rather than by any flaw in the metrics themselves. We take this as motivation for validating drift metrics against a measured ground truth---for instance, using a dataset's internal, labeled sub-domains (such as MNLI's five annotated genres) to obtain a directly measured transfer-difficulty ground truth against which candidate metrics can be checked---before trusting any of them on benchmarks, like ANLI and WANLI, where the true magnitude of shift is not independently known. We have not yet conducted this controlled validation; we describe it as the most important next step in Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

This paper reports a diagnostic, methodological study, and we scope our claims accordingly:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{What we claim, and what we do not.} We claim that MMD and Sinkhorn drift profiles disagree in a domain-dependent way and that drift magnitude dissociates from adaptation difficulty on at least one benchmark (ANLI R3); we do \emph{not} claim that drift-guided LoRA allocation helps or hurts downstream fine-tuning. The single-seed, two-of-four-target PEFT result in Table~\ref{tab:peft} was a sanity check consistent with this scope, not an independently powered benchmark comparison, and we treat it as such: a 3-seed, all-four-target extension of this matrix is in progress at the time of writing, and Table~\ref{tab:peft} will be reported with mean and standard deviation across seeds, not a single run, before this paper is submitted.
\item \textbf{No controlled ground-truth validation of the drift metrics yet.} Our validity evidence for MMD and Sinkhorn is currently limited to stability (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-b}) and cross-metric correlation on real, uncontrolled target domains, not a direct measurement of either metric's correspondence to true adaptation difficulty. We propose MNLI's five annotated genres (fiction, government, slate, telephone, travel) as a controlled internal testbed for this: measured cross-genre transfer-accuracy degradation would give a ground truth against which MMD, Sinkhorn, CKA, and a classifier-based $\mathcal{H}$-divergence \cite{bendavid2010theory} could all be checked on equal footing (Sec.~\ref{sec:related} explains our reservation about CKA specifically) before any of them is trusted on WANLI/ANLI. This is, in our view, the most important next step and is not yet run.
\item \textbf{Benchmark framing.} WANLI and ANLI are stylistic and adversarially-constructed shifts within a single general NLI task, not shifts across semantically distinct domains (e.g., legal, biomedical, financial). We describe this work as addressing \emph{distribution shift} adaptation rather than \emph{domain} adaptation in the cross-topic sense, and do not present WANLI/ANLI as domain-adaptation benchmarks without this qualification.
\item \textbf{AdaLoRA comparison: in progress, with a disclosed budget caveat.} Unlike the other limitations above, we did not leave this unattempted: a budget-matched AdaLoRA baseline (Sec.~\ref{sec:budget-verification}) is implemented, verified, and running at the time of writing, using the closest achievable parameter match (888{,}003 effective vs.\ 887{,}811 for every other method, a disclosed 0.02\% structural overhead---Sec.~\ref{sec:budget-verification}). Results will be added to Table~\ref{tab:peft} before submission; this paper does not yet contain them.
\item \textbf{A causal, rather than correlational, test of layer capacity}---boosting or cutting rank at a single, specifically chosen layer and measuring the resulting accuracy change---would further sharpen this diagnostic picture. It is not required to support the claims we actually make, and is not attempted in this paper.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

This paper is a diagnostic, negative-result study, not a new state-of-the-art method: we present a rigorous, reproducible methodology for measuring layer-wise representation drift in a frozen transformer language model, and use it to drive a verified, parameter-budget-matched LoRA rank-allocation pipeline. Our central empirical findings are about the measurement itself: MMD and Sinkhorn, two established distributional-distance metrics, disagree with each other in a domain-dependent way that is sensitive to representation-pooling methodology, and drift magnitude does not reliably track adaptation difficulty, particularly for adversarially-constructed benchmarks. A diagnostic sanity check---a single-seed application of drift-guided allocation to real LoRA training---shows no detectable advantage over uniform allocation, which is consistent with, and reinforces, the measurement-level finding rather than an independent result we claim stronger support for. We argue that establishing \emph{which} drift metric, if any, can be trusted---ideally via controlled validation against a directly measured ground truth---should precede, and is a prerequisite for, any claim that drift-guided allocation improves parameter-efficient adaptation in practice.
